The Legend of the Pirate Lodge

Note: Written by Grok and posted at Gregg's request. Gregg hasn't read or edited it yet.

Gregg Oberg & Grok · November 15, 2026

On a lodge night, a good legend does two jobs at once. It gives the brethren something worth telling, and it tells the truth about what is known and what is only hoped. Harmony Lodge No. 9 has been half-jokingly talking about starting a pirate lodge. Here is the story it can stand on: that the black flag and the Craft grew up in the same salt air, in the same decade, and that a brother who can tell a document from a yarn is the best kind of shipmate.

The same decade

The Golden Age of piracy is usually dated from about 1715 to 1726 — the years of Nassau, of Bartholomew Roberts, of the Jolly Roger as the world still pictures it. They are also the years when modern Freemasonry found its public footing. The first Grand Lodge was formed in London in 1717. Anderson's Constitutions followed in 1723. Two worlds, one stretch of water and time. That overlap is on the record. It is not proof that one invented the other. It is a coincidence rich enough to found a story on, if we are honest about what it is and is not.

The articles and the Charges

Marcus Rediker's Villains of All Nations and Peter Leeson's work on pirate “constitutions” describe how many Golden Age crews ran themselves. Captains were often elected. Written articles set the shares. A man who lost a limb might be paid from the common chest. A Mason hearing that list will feel a familiar breeze: the old Charges speak of equality among brethren, of officers chosen by the lodge, and of charity. It is a fine comparison for a talk after supper, not a documented borrowing. Nobody has shown that pirate articles came out of a lodge, or the other way around. The pirate lodge can love the rhyme without forging the receipt.

The skull

The same picture has flown over two very different decks. In a lodge, a skull is a public reminder of mortality: a memento mori, a call to use the time we have well. On a pirate mast, the skull and crossbones was a terror flag, meant to end a fight before it started. The Grand Lodge of British Columbia and Yukon has a short paper on this point. There is no evidence that pirates took the emblem from Masons, and the story of a Templar pirate flag is a legend.

A brother the British called a pirate

If this lodge wants a real seafarer who was both a Mason and, to his enemies, a pirate, the record points to John Paul Jones — not to Blackbeard or Roberts. On November 27, 1770, at St. Bernard's Lodge in Kirkcudbright, Scotland, John Paul, then commander of the John, was made a Mason. The petition and the lodge minute still exist. In 1780, the Lodge of the Nine Sisters in Paris received him, and commissioned Jean-Antoine Houdon's bust of him. The British called him a pirate. He was a naval officer and a Mason. He was not a Golden Age rover, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Lodges at sea

Masonry did go to sea, and not only in stories. In 1760, Thomas Dunckerley, master gunner of HMS Vanguard, received a warrant to hold a lodge aboard that ship — thought to be the first English lodge on a man-of-war. He later formed lodges on HMS Prince and HMS Guadeloupe. The Vanguard lodge came ashore in 1768 and lives on as London Lodge. A pirate lodge, for now, is a joke with a straight face. A warranted lodge on a Royal Navy ship is a matter of record.

Two yarns from later years

The lodge-night stories that tie Masons to pirates come later, and they should be told as anecdotes, not as minutes. In 1795, according to a nineteenth-century account later retold in a Grand Lodge of Alberta bulletin, the American ship Betsy was taken by a Tripolitan raider. Captain Chapin Sampson of Maine and his crew were carried into Tripoli and set to hard labor. A Tripolitan officer named Hassan Bey, said to have been made a Mason in France, recognized the captain as a brother, saw to his comfort, and helped him leave. Whether every detail holds, we cannot say.

Another, told in 1845 at a London lodge of instruction and set about fifteen years earlier — so around 1830 — describes a merchant ship bound for Cuba, taken and prepared for burning, until a Masonic appeal was recognized by one of the company and the crew was spared. We will not dress that story up with private signs or passwords. We will only say what the tellers claimed: that a bond of brotherhood, even on a hostile deck, was enough to stay a hand. Treat both as lodge-night yarns.

The names that will not quite hold

Jean Lafitte is often pulled into this company. Writers have said his brothers were Masons, and a later book argues that he faked his death and helped found a lodge in North Carolina. That theory is disputed, and there is no solid record that Jean himself was a Mason. As for Blackbeard, Bartholomew Roberts, Calico Jack, and Anne Bonny, we have not found a single famous Golden Age pirate who is documented as a Mason. The other temptation is a Templar fleet sailing from the Temple to the Caribbean. It makes a thrilling chapter in books such as Ernesto Frers's The Templar Pirates. Historians treat that story as legend, and so should we.

What we will found the lodge on

The pirate lodge can take the documented crossing as its keel, the rhymes as its sail, and the myths as yarns to examine, not to swallow. That is a founding legend a lodge can be proud of: not that pirates were Masons, but that men who lived by a code, who chose their officers, who looked after their injured, and who kept a reminder of mortality close at hand, have always been in the same waters as the Craft. We will tell it that way, and we will tell the difference.

Fact or legend?

  • Documented. The 1715–1726 / 1717 / 1723 overlap; John Paul Jones at Kirkcudbright (1770) and the Nine Sisters (1780); Dunckerley's 1760 Vanguard warrant and later ship lodges; the skull as a reminder of mortality in a lodge and, separately, as a pirate flag — with no proven descent from one to the other.
  • A useful analogy, not a proven link. Pirate articles and the Masonic Charges. The rhyme is real. A borrowing is not documented.
  • Lodge-night anecdotes, not minutes. Tripoli, 1795 (Captain Chapin Sampson and Hassan Bey); a merchant ship bound for Cuba, about 1830.
  • Legend, or unsupported. That any famous Golden Age pirate was a Mason; that pirates took the skull from Freemasonry or the Templars; that Jean Lafitte was a Mason or later helped found a North Carolina lodge; that a Templar pirate fleet ties the two histories together.
