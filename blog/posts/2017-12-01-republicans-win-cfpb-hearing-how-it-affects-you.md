<p><em><strong>Article By: Ben Giumarra, Spillane Consulting Associates</strong></em></p>
<p><em>What you should to know about the CFPB turmoil and how it affects you</em></p>
<p class="font_8"><strong>The Debate</strong></p>
<p class="font_8">Mr. Cordray left a vacancy when he stepped down as director of the CFPB. The big question remaining &#8211; who will serve as acting director until a replacement can go through the appointment process (which can take years)? President Trump claimed authority to assign his choice (Mulvaney) pursuant to a general law giving authority to fill vacancies in such administrative positions. But the current deputy director (English) filed a lawsuit claiming she is the rightful replacement and that the Dodd Frank Act specifically requires the deputy director to take over if the director leaves.</p>
<p class="font_8">So there is a conflict between an older general law and a newer, more specific law.</p>
<p class="font_8"><b>Nov. 28th Hearing: Trump Wins</b></p>
<p class="font_8">Yesterday a Federal court in D.C. held a special hearing to settle this issue (although this decision could be appealed). In that hearing, the judge ruled in favor of President Trump. So Mulvaney is acting director and English is deputy director (although probably not for long).</p>
<p class="font_8"><b>What this means for you</b></p>
<p class="font_8">The director of the CFPB, even an interim director, has significant power to reshape the agency&#8217;s enforcement, rulemaking, and other activities.</p>
<p class="font_8"><em>Enforcement.</em></p>
<p class="font_8">The acting director can make it significantly more difficult for CFPB examiners to take enforcement action, which predominantly affects the largest lending institutions and those in the CFPB&#8217;s crosshairs for other reasons. Think Santander. It might very well be a lucky break for organizations in the midst of dealing trouble with the agency. The director has broad powers to prevent enforcement action from being taken, and even then to limit the penalties imposed.</p>
<p class="font_8">I&#8217;m not sure how much help this will be for the vast majority of other institutions. For those that see CFPB enforcement just as leveling the playing field, removing the CFPB&#8217;s enforcement threat just leaves them to deal with the same state regulatory agencies as before. Perhaps these state agencies are even bolstered by the CFPB&#8217;s struggles.</p>
<p class="font_8"><em>Rulemaking &amp; Guidance</em></p>
<p class="font_8">The CFPB is required by Dodd Frank to do certain things. But the director can slow things down. The most interesting issue to me right now is HMDA. Had Trump lost this battle, certainly a Trump appointee wouldn&#8217;t have been in place in time to disrupt implementation of the upcoming HMDA rules. some of these down, but probably can&#8217;t completely stop all of them. HMDA is an interesting issue because, while Dodd Frank does require that the CFPB implement changes here, the CFPB has the power to delay implementation. In fact, industry representatives have <a href="http://www.cutimes.com/2017/11/27/cuna-nafcu-assume-mulvaney-is-boss-at-cfpb-send-hi" target="_blank" rel="noopener" data-content="http://www.cutimes.com/2017/11/27/cuna-nafcu-assume-mulvaney-is-boss-at-cfpb-send-hi" data-type="external">already written Mulvaney asking for a 1-year delay</a>. The CFPB could also rewrite many (but not all) of the planned regulatory changes, intending to make the HMDA rule less burdensome.</p>
<p class="font_8">If we do go ahead with HMDA implementation as planned, let&#8217;s at least hope that the CFPB is allowed to continue issuing clarifying guidance, as promised, because for now CFPB activities are frozen.</p>
<p class="font_8"><em>States and Other Regulators</em></p>
<p class="font_8">The CFPB has ruled as king of all other regulators for the past several years, with Federal and state regulatory bodies following along with almost all of the CFPB&#8217;s rulemaking and guidance initiatives. But it can&#8217;t undo everything it has created by itself.</p>
<p class="font_8">Will the FDIC, Federal Reserve, state enforcement agencies, etc. start to break ranks if politics derails the CFPB? This might be seen especially with state requirements. Yes, the CFPB wrote the Ability to Repay Rule. It&#8217;s a Federal regulation. But then states such as Massachusetts copied it and made it a state law. So even if the CFPB can rewrite the Ability to Repay or other regulations, or just stops enforcing it, much of the regulatory regime put in place over the past several years can&#8217;t be undone very easily.</p>

<p class="font_8"><strong>On My Mind &#8230;</strong></p>
<p class="font_8">What&#8217;s the key to empowerment and employee engagement? Who knows! There probably is no single key. But one thing some companies do is give employees time to work on their own ideas;freedom to innovate. <em><strong>3M for example, has had such a policy of&#8221;15% time&#8221; in place since 1948</strong></em>. This <a href="http://archive.fortune.com/2010/09/23/news/companies/3m_innovation_revival.fortune/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener" data-content="http://archive.fortune.com/2010/09/23/news/companies/3m_innovation_revival.fortune/index.htm" data-type="external">has produced some of its best inventions</a> (including sticky notes). Google, who used to have a similar policy, also benefited from a surprising number of inventions, including the wildly popular G-Mail. According to 3M: <em>&#8220;If there&#8217;s a secret ingredient to 3M&#8217;s more than 100 years of innovation, it&#8217;s this: give talented people the time and resources to prove the worth of their ideas, and in the long run you&#8217;ll come out well ahead. Even if those talented people are mistaken, you&#8217;ve learned something.&#8221;</em></p>
