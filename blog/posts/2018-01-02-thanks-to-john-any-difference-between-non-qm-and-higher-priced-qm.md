<p><em><strong>Article By: Ben Giumarra, Spillane Consulting Associates, Inc.</strong></em></p>
<p><em>When regulatory requirements impact strategic or management action, our goal is to help business leaders make well-informed decisions.</em></p>
<p class="font_8">Many institutions have avoided the potential risks of making non-qualified mortgage loans. Of course, there&#8217;s no universally correct answer to the question of whether to engage in non-QM lending. But as a consultant, the best I can hope for is that business leaders are well-advised when considering these types of decisions.</p>
<p class="font_8">In this pursuit, I have a question when looking at industry strategy on non-QM issues &#8211; Why do some organizations with strict non-QM policies freely engage in higher-priced mortgage lending? Similarly, why do some organizations have extra safeguards (or higher pricing) for non-QM loans that don&#8217;t apply to higher-priced loans with QM status?</p>
<p class="font_8">I wonder this because, from my perspective, the risk of a non-QM is fairly similar to the risk of a QM that happens to be &#8220;higher-priced.&#8221;</p>
<p class="font_8"><strong>The Risk of Non-QM: Endless (and expensive) Argument</strong></p>
<p class="font_8">The general ability to repay (ATR) rule requires that a lender &#8220;makes a reasonable and good faith determination … that the consumer will have a reasonable ability to repay the loan.&#8221;</p>
<p class="font_8">This is a scary argument to win against a borrower as the preemptively evil financial institution. Who knows what an individual judge or jury is going to think is &#8220;reasonable&#8221; or in &#8220;good faith&#8221;. Yikes! Mortgage underwriting isn&#8217;t something taught in high school courses anyway, but even if you get an educated group, there&#8217;s so much subjectivity here. Seems impossible to win easily without a big debate application.</p>
<p class="font_8"><strong>QM Safe Haven</strong></p>
<p class="font_8">So the safe haven that QM provides from this is that, if you originate a loan with certain extra safe features (enough to make it a &#8220;qualified mortgage&#8221;), then a consumer cannot even raise the ATR argument, i.e., they never get to argue over whether the lender complied with the ATR rule. Instead it&#8217;s simple &#8211; did you pay your loan? No? Well then, you should. (And even here we already find foreclosure and mortgage actions frustratingly difficult to litigate).</p>
<p class="font_8"><b>Higher-Priced QM vs. Non-QM</b></p>
<p class="font_8">But remember that QM provides a different level of protection for &#8220;higher-priced&#8221; loans. A higher-priced loan can still be a QM, but receives less protection.</p>
<p class="font_8">Specifically, the rule provides for a &#8220;safe harbor for loans that are not higher-priced covered transactions&#8221; and that QM status is definitive proof that a lender &#8220;complies with the repayment ability requirements of [the ATR rule].&#8221; TILA 1026.43€ (1)(i). But there is merely a &#8220;presumption of compliance for higher-priced covered transactions&#8221; and here a lender is &#8220;presumed to comply the repayment ability requirements of [the ATR rule]&#8221; but the consumer has a chance to &#8220;rebut the presumption&#8221; by providing evidence that the lender &#8220;did not have a reasonable and good faith belief in the consumer&#8217;s repayment ability.&#8221;</p>
<p class="font_8">If that sounds kind of circular, you&#8217;re right. The argument that will occur over a higher-priced QM is very similar to that of a non-QM. The only difference from a non-QM is that a court is supposed to require a higher level of proof to prove the ATR argument. In my opinion, that&#8217;s not a big help &#8211; it&#8217;s a tool for the lender&#8217;s lawyer to use in court, but, as we all know &#8211; if we have to take a case to court, then in many cases we&#8217;ve already lost &#8211; no matter the legal outcome.</p>
<p class="font_8">So in conclusion, that&#8217;s <strong>why I think lenders should consider higher-priced QMs to be just as risky as non-QMs</strong>. Now, that might mean toughening up on higher-priced QM underwriting. But it might just as likely show a lender that its non-QM underwriting is too conservative. I&#8217;m not here to say which is right for your organization, just want you to make well-informed decisions.</p>
